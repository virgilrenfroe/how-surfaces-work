How Glass Bends What You See

Light slows in denser materials and bends at the surface. Water, glass, and diamond each warp the world behind them by a different amount.

Time 20 min
You will learn Refraction · IOR · thickness

Core lesson Higher IOR bends more

What you will see

IORHow strongly a material bends light. Water about 1.33. Glass about 1.5. Diamond about 2.4.
ThicknessA thicker lens magnifies and warps the background more.
DispersionDifferent colors bend by slightly different amounts. A prism can show a spectrum.
TIRAt a steep exit angle, light can bounce inside a dense gem instead of leaving.
IOR · grid warp

Specimen 01 — Index of refraction

Air to diamond. Watch the grid bend.

After Fresnel tells you how much light reflects, refraction is about the light that goes through and changes direction.

Raise the index of refraction and the checker behind the sphere warps more. That is Snell’s law in a picture.

Try this

Drag IOR from near 1.0 up toward 2.4. Then tap the presets for Water, Glass, and Diamond.

Material: Glass · n ≈ 1.50

Lens · thickness

Specimen 02 — Thickness

A thicker lens warps more

Curvature and path length through the glass both matter. A thin sheet barely shifts the grid. A thick lens magnifies it.

Keep IOR fixed and only change thickness so you see one cause at a time.

Try this

Slide Thickness from thin to thick. Watch the grid behind the lens grow and bend.

Prism · dispersion

Specimen 03 — Dispersion

Colors bend by slightly different amounts

A prism can split white light because shorter wavelengths usually bend a little more than longer ones.

Turn Dispersion on for a subtle RGB fringe. Keep it soft so it stays truthful, not cartoon.

Try this

Press Dispersion on, then off. Compare the soft spectrum fringe along the prism edges.

Gem · TIR

Specimen 04 — Total internal reflection

Same cut. Different sparkle.

When light inside a dense material hits the surface at a steep angle, it can bounce back instead of leaving. That is total internal reflection.

Diamond’s high IOR makes that bounce more likely than glass of the same shape, which is one reason a well-cut gem holds light.

Try this

Toggle Glass and Diamond. Watch how the high-IOR gem keeps brighter internal bounce.

After the lab

Where you see this

Refraction is everyday craft for people who light glass, liquid, bottles, headlights, and façades.

  1. 01

    Glass and liquid in film and games

    Look-development artist · lighting artist

    Look-development and lighting artists set IOR and thickness so windows, bottles, and oceans bend the scene behind them. Without refraction, clear materials look like painted plastic.

  2. 02

    Bottles, perfume, and drinkware online

    3D product visualization artist

    Product visualization artists rely on transmission and IOR so glass bottles magnify labels and liquid volume reads as real. Clients judge those stills against photography before a mold is cut.

  3. 03

    Headlights and windshields in car renders

    Automotive CGI artist

    Automotive CGI artists model headlight lenses and windshields with accurate IOR so beams and cabin views bend the right way. That glass work is part of every exterior and night lighting pass.

  4. 04

    Storefront and façade glass in architecture images

    Archviz artist

    Archviz artists give storefronts and curtain walls enough refraction and thickness that street life warps gently through the glass. Flat opacity alone makes a building look like a solid block.

  5. 05

    Measuring refractive index in the lab

    Optical engineer · materials scientist

    Optical engineers and materials scientists measure refractive index for glass, plastics, and coatings. Those measured n values are the same numbers students dial on the IOR slider here.

For teachers

Short objectives, one classroom loop, and how refraction maps to studio and science practice.

Learning objectives

  • State that denser materials bend light more (higher IOR).
  • Recall water ≈ 1.33, glass ≈ 1.5, diamond ≈ 2.4.
  • Explain why thickness and curvature increase the warp.
  • Describe dispersion as wavelength-dependent bend, kept subtle.

10-minute classroom activity

1) Look through a drinking glass at lined paper. 2) Tip the glass and note the warp. 3) Match it on Specimen 01 by raising IOR. 4) On Specimen 02, write one sentence on why a thicker lens magnifies more.
